Sustainabledesign

In the tropics, most of what makes a building sustainable is decided before any equipment: where it sits, which way it faces, how deep its roof is. We design to those decisions from the first visit, and certify them with RESET or LEED® if you want the label.

(Strategies)

  1. (01) Climate

    Sun, shade and breeze

    The cheapest energy is the energy a building never needs. We draw the sun path and the prevailing wind for every site, then place the rooms, size the overhangs and open the walls so that shade and cross ventilation do most of the cooling.

    • Sun path and shade study
    • Overhangs sized to the sun
    • Cross ventilation through every room
    • Insulated roofs
    • Daylight without glare
    • Air conditioning only where it is needed
    The climate of each town we work in →

  2. (02) Water

    Rain kept for the dry season

    Rain comes in a few months and in large amounts, and in Guanacaste the dry season lasts about five. We size cisterns to the roof and to those months, send grey water to the garden where the rules allow it, and plant so the garden gets through the dry season on little irrigation.

    • Rainwater harvesting sized to the roof
    • Cisterns for irrigation and toilets
    • Grey water reuse where allowed
    • Low-flow fixtures
    • Drip irrigation
    • Rain that soaks into the lot instead of running off

  3. (03) Materials

    Built for salt air

    On the coast, a material that fails in ten years is the least sustainable choice. We choose for salt air, humidity and sun: plantation teak, board-formed concrete, local stone and finishes that age instead of peeling, bought close to the site where we can.

    • Plantation and certified timber
    • Board-formed concrete
    • Local stone
    • Paints and sealers low in solvents
    • Hardware that resists salt air
    • Suppliers close to the site

  4. (04) Energy

    Little left to run

    What is left to run should run on little: ceiling fans before air conditioning, LED lighting with controls, hot water from the sun, and roofs oriented and built to carry solar panels, now or later.

    • Ceiling fans
    • Split air conditioning in bedrooms only
    • LED lighting with sensors and timers
    • Solar water heating
    • Roofs ready for solar panels
    • Efficient appliances

  5. (05) Landscape

    Keep what grows there

    We survey the trees before drawing and design around the ones worth keeping. New planting uses native species in layers (canopy, shrubs and ground cover), which need less water and feed the birds and insects of the place. Near nesting beaches, like Playa Grande, outdoor lights stay low and shielded from the sand.

    • Tree survey before the design
    • Native species
    • Layered planting
    • Lighting that stays off the beach and the sky
    • Native ground cover instead of lawn
    • Compost for garden waste

  6. (06) Planted roofs

    The roof as a garden

    A planted roof keeps the room below cooler, holds back the rain during a storm and gives the floors above something green to look at. Kauhane House has a planted terrace on its upper floor.

    • Planted roofs and terraces
    • Drought-tolerant native plants
    • Drainage sized for tropical storms
    • Waterproofing with a root barrier

(How it runs)

  1. (01)

    Choose the standard

    With the lot study or the concept

    We compare RESET and LEED for your project's type, budget and goals, set the level to aim for, and register the project with the certifier.

    You receive

    • Which standard and which level
    • What certification will cost, certifier included
    • The list of criteria the design has to meet
  2. (02)

    Design to the criteria

    With the design

    Orientation, shade, cross ventilation, water and materials are decided against the criteria and recorded as we draw. RESET reviews design, construction and operation as separate stages 1; an energy model is added where the standard asks for one.

    You receive

    • The criteria tracked in the drawings
    • The design documentation for review
  3. (03)

    Build and verify

    During construction

    We keep the evidence the standard asks for on site: materials, waste, water and energy systems. LEED houses need on-site verification by an independent Green Rater from the design stage on 6.

    You receive

    • Evidence for each criterion
    • Site verification where it applies
  4. (04)

    Certification

    After the build

    We submit the project to INTECO or GBCI and answer the reviewers until the certificate is issued.

    You receive

    • The certificate and its level

(Fees)

Certification adds two costs to the design: our work on it, agreed up front as a separate fee, and the certifier's fees, which the owner pays. For LEED these are a registration fee and a certification fee set by GBCI by floor area 4; for RESET, the evaluation by INTECO 1. An energy model or commissioning, where the standard requires them, are paid to those specialists.

The decisions the standards reward, deep shade, rooms that cross-ventilate, water that is collected and reused, are ones we already make for the climate. Most of the extra cost is the evidence and the review, not the building.

(Questions)

RESET or LEED, which one in Costa Rica?

RESET was written in Costa Rica for tropical buildings, is published as the national standard INTE C170 and is evaluated by INTECO 1. LEED is international and better known to foreign buyers, guests and investors, and it costs more to certify. For a house or a small hotel RESET usually fits; for a hotel aimed at international guests or a brand, LEED may be worth it.

Can a house be LEED certified?

Yes, under the LEED residential rating systems. The difference is a third party: an independent Green Rater has to verify the house on site, from the design stage to the end of the build 6.

Who grants the certificate?

Not the architect. For RESET, INTECO evaluates the project against INTE C170 1; for LEED, GBCI reviews it on behalf of the U.S. Green Building Council 2. A project that is only registered is not certified, and we never describe it as if it were.

When should we decide?

Before the concept. The standards reward decisions about where the building sits and which way it faces, and those are the first ones we make. Adding certification once the plans are approved is possible, but usually costs more and reaches a lower level.
